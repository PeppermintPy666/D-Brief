The Iran-Hormuz dynamic is the swing variable. Brent at $96.80 is down from March peaks above $113 but still 47% above year-ago levels. If peace negotiations produce a durable deal, oil could retrace toward $80, unwinding the inflation shock that pushed Brazil’s Focus IPCA above ceiling. This would give the BCB clear air to continue its easing cycle — paradoxically, a peace deal that allows faster SELIC cuts could eventually weaken BRL as the carry erodes. In the near term, though, peace = risk-on = BRL stronger.

The fiscal fault line is the medium-term threat. The market is not pricing fiscal risk right now (CDS at 138 bps is remarkably calm for a country with rising debt/GDP and a questionable primary surplus target). As the easing cycle progresses and real rates normalise, the carry cushion thins, and fiscal fundamentals will reassert themselves. The transition from “carry trade darling” to “fiscal reality check” is a matter of when, not if — likely H2 2026.

Media narratives don’t move currencies directly. The framing a capital pool absorbs determines its risk appetite, time horizon, and directional bias.

SECTION 4 — 3-Point Stress-Test

Monetarist Lens

Data inputs: Money supply growth in Brazil (M2) has moderated; BCB’s hawkish posture through 2025 constrained credit growth. SELIC at 14.75% is deeply restrictive. US M2 has stabilised after 2022-2024 tightening. The rate differential of 1,100 bps reflects relative monetary tightness — Brazil’s real rate is ~10% (SELIC 14.75% minus IPCA 4.71%), among the highest globally.

Prediction: BRL should appreciate as long as real rates remain deeply positive and money supply growth is controlled. The monetarist framework strongly favours BRL. Inflation expectations rising above target (Focus at 4.71%) is a concern — if the BCB cuts too fast, real rates compress and the carry unwinds. But at 14.75%, there is enormous buffer.

Reality check: BRL at R$4.97 is consistent with the monetarist prediction. The carry trade is the dominant mechanism, and it is working as predicted. However, monetarism struggles with the geopolitical supply shock (oil) — it treats inflation as always a monetary phenomenon, but the Hormuz disruption is a real supply constraint that no interest rate can resolve.

Score: 8/10 — Strong explanatory power for current BRL levels; weakens if supply-side inflation forces BCB’s hand.

Post-Keynesian Lens

Data inputs: Brazil’s fiscal deficit is widening (debt/GDP 78.6%, primary surplus target under strain). Government spending is maintaining aggregate demand. The $11.7B in foreign equity inflows represent financial capital, not productive investment — a Minskian concern. IBOVESPA at 18 ATHs suggests speculative positioning. The carry trade itself is a form of financial fragility: borrowed USD converted to BRL for yield, creating a latent short-USD position that unwinds violently in a crisis.

Prediction: The current BRL strength is a financial cycle, not a fundamental equilibrium. The carry trade is building fragility — when it reverses (triggered by geopolitical shock, fiscal crisis, or rate convergence), the unwind will be sharp and procyclical. Post-Keynesians would warn that the “wall of money” flowing into Brazil is hot money that leaves faster than it arrives. The fiscal trajectory (debt rising, primary surplus questionable) is the structural vulnerability that will eventually reassert.

Reality check: This lens correctly identifies the fragility, but has been wrong on timing — the BRL has appreciated for 6 months while post-Keynesians would have been cautious. The carry trade has not yet shown signs of Minsky instability. However, the 7 convergent bullish narratives identified in Section 3 are exactly the kind of consensus-crowding that precedes a Minsky moment.

Score: 6/10 — Correctly identifies structural risks but wrong on timing; its time may come if ceasefire collapses or fiscal data disappoints.

Structuralist/Dependency Lens

Data inputs: Brazil remains a commodity exporter whose terms of trade are set by global demand (China for iron ore, global food markets for soy). The carry trade attracts financial capital but doesn’t transform Brazil’s productive structure. Debt/GDP is rising because the fiscal model depends on commodity revenues that fluctuate with global cycles. The R$4.97 exchange rate, while strong, creates Dutch Disease dynamics — making Brazilian manufactured exports uncompetitive while boosting import purchasing power.

Prediction: BRL appreciation hurts Brazil’s industrial base (what remains of it) while benefiting commodity exporters and financial asset holders. The carry-driven capital inflow increases dependence on foreign portfolio capital that can exit instantaneously. When the cycle turns (commodity downturn, carry reversal, or fiscal crisis), Brazil will face the classic EM bind: currency collapse, capital flight, and austerity — repeating the pattern of the 1990s, 2002, and 2015-16. Structural transformation requires a weaker, more competitive BRL — the current strength is a symptom of financialisation, not development.

Reality check: The structuralist lens is analytically powerful for medium-term framing but operationally unhelpful for the next 30 days. BRL will not weaken because the carry is wrong on structuralist terms; it will weaken when a specific trigger (Iran, fiscal, Fed) forces the hot money out. The structuralist contribution is explaining why, when the reversal comes, it will be severe.

Score: 5/10 — Strongest analytical frame for medium-to-long-term, weakest for near-term prediction. Underpredicts the duration of carry-driven rallies.

The key divergence is on what constitutes a “correction”: - Monetarist: BRL weakens only if BCB cuts too aggressively or Fed hikes - Post-Keynesian: BRL weakens when the carry trade reverses (Minsky moment) - Structuralist: BRL weakens when the commodity cycle turns or fiscal model breaks

These are not mutually exclusive — the most dangerous scenario is one where all three triggers align.

FOR THE ECON STUDENT — Key Concepts

Terms Used in This Briefing

  1. Carry Trade — Borrowing in a low-interest-rate currency (USD at 3.625%) and investing in a high-interest-rate currency (BRL at 14.75%) to earn the spread. The risk: if the high-yield currency depreciates by more than the interest differential, the trade loses money. Brazil’s 1,100 bps spread is among the widest globally, making it a carry trade magnet — but also fragile if sentiment turns.

  2. Minsky Moment — Named after economist Hyman Minsky. The point at which excessive speculative borrowing triggers a sudden collapse in asset prices. In this context: the crowded BRL carry trade, where 7 of 10 media frames align bullish, represents the kind of consensus positioning that precedes Minsky-type reversals.

  3. Dutch Disease — When a country’s currency appreciates due to natural resource exports or capital inflows, making its manufactured exports uncompetitive. BRL at R$4.97 (strongest since 2024) raises this concern for Brazilian industry.

  4. Terms of Trade — The ratio of a country’s export prices to its import prices. Brazil’s terms of trade are improving (high soy/iron ore/oil prices) but the Hormuz disruption complicates this for energy imports.

  5. Real Interest Rate — The nominal interest rate minus expected inflation. Brazil’s real SELIC rate is ~10% (14.75% minus IPCA 4.71%), making it one of the highest in the world and the primary driver of BRL strength.

  6. Credit Default Swap (CDS) — A financial derivative that acts as insurance against a country defaulting on its debt. Brazil’s 5Y CDS at 138.75 bps means it costs $138,750 per year to insure $10 million of Brazilian government debt — a moderate risk premium.

  7. Forward Guidance — Central bank communication about future policy intentions. Both the BCB and Fed are in “data-dependent” mode, meaning their guidance is conditional on incoming inflation and growth data — creating uncertainty that markets must price.

  8. Convergence Risk — When multiple independent signals (media narratives, capital flows, positioning data) all point in the same direction, the apparent consensus can mask fragility. If the consensus is wrong, everyone exits simultaneously.

Suggested Reading

  1. “Stabilizing an Unstable Economy” by Hyman P. Minsky — The foundational text on financial fragility and why periods of apparent stability (like BRL’s current rally) breed the conditions for instability. Essential for understanding the post-Keynesian lens applied in Section 4.

  2. “The Dollar Trap” by Eswar S. Prasad — Explains why the USD maintains its safe-haven status even when US fundamentals deteriorate, and why EM currencies like BRL are structurally vulnerable to dollar-demand shocks. Directly relevant to the Fed standpoint analysis.

  3. “The New Economics of Sovereign Debt” by Eduardo Fernandez-Arias & Eduardo Levy-Yeyati (IDB Working Paper) — Short, accessible paper on how fiscal sustainability interacts with exchange rate dynamics in Latin American economies. Provides the theoretical framework for understanding why Brazil’s debt/GDP trajectory matters for BRL even when carry is attractive.
